# A reckoning — asking your graph what to do

A reckoning is what this product is named after, and it is the thing you do once you have a graph worth asking. You describe the situation you are in and the outcome you want, and you get back options that are built ONLY from facts you have confirmed — each one showing which of your own facts it stands on. The name is the old sense of the word: to reckon is to work something out from what you have, and to be held to account for the answer.

<p class="in-sets">Part of Ask it things.</p>

## At a glance

**Audience**

Anyone who has been told this is a note-taking tool and is wondering what it is actually for.

## Why it is this way

**Principle**

- EVERY OPTION SHOWS ITS WORKING. An option that cites three facts you recognise is one you can act on. An option that cites a fact you had forgotten is the useful kind of surprise. An option that cites nothing does not appear, because a proposal with no grounding is a guess with better formatting.
- IT CANNOT USE A FACT YOU HAVE NOT ACCEPTED. That is the entire difference between this and asking a chatbot the same question. A chatbot answers from everything it has ever read plus whatever you pasted; a reckoning answers from your confirmed graph and nothing else, which is why it can tell you it does not have enough to go on instead of producing a confident answer built on nothing.

**Constraint**

- IT IS ONLY AS GOOD AS WHAT YOU PUT IN, and the product does not pretend otherwise. A reckoning over a graph of nine facts will say very little of value. This is the honest cost of the approach: the review step that makes the answer trustworthy is also the step that makes it slow to get started, and no amount of automation removes it without also removing the reason to believe the result.
- IT PROPOSES. YOU DECIDE. A reckoning does not act, schedule, send, or change anything — it hands you options and the reasoning behind them. Nothing in this product does something on your behalf because it concluded that it should.

## What we found

**Example**

SITUATION: our staging deploys have failed twice this month, both times after a dependency bump, and the person who set the pipeline up has left. TARGET: stop the failures without rebuilding the pipeline. What comes back is a handful of options assembled from what your graph already holds about those deploys — and each one names the facts it used, so you can see whether it read the situation correctly before you act on it.
