# A reckoning — asking your graph what to do

> **Production** — built, tested, and in use.

A reckoning is what this product is named after, and it is the thing you do once you have a graph worth asking. You describe the situation you are in and the outcome you want, and you get back options that are built ONLY from facts you have confirmed — each one showing which of your own facts it stands on. The name is the old sense of the word: to reckon is to work something out from what you have, and to be held to account for the answer.

<p class="derived">This is built, working, and in daily use here.</p>

<p class="in-sets">Part of Ask it things.</p>

THE FLOW HAS FOUR STEPS, AND EACH STEP LEAVES SOMETHING BEHIND YOU CAN COME BACK TO. First you choose the situation: the Reckoning page lists your statements by standing — the ones you hold, the ones still pending, the ones you are still asking — with search, and you pick which of them the reckoning should reason from. You can also select things on the canvas and ask to 'Reckon with these' (it takes the statements that touch them, up to 40, the held ones first), or add a sentence of your own on top. Then you choose the target: an open question you want answered, an asset you want made (an image, a video, a 3D model, or a splat), or words of your own. Then you ask for the proposal. It comes back as two to four options, each one written down as pending so you can look it over: the statements each option would add (drawn dashed on the canvas), the assets each option would like to make (listed, not queued — a job in a proposed state is not read by anything that runs work), the places where a citation is still missing (left as open questions with a blank), and the statements each option rests on. A reply that is not in the agreed shape is refused and nothing is written; a model that cannot give the shape at all falls back to a plain summary that says so, and it also writes nothing. Last, you decide: each option has Accept and Reject buttons that ask first and say what will happen. Accepting an option confirms its statements, hands its asset jobs to your connected generation tool in one approval, and marks the other options as superseded (they stay readable); questions an option left open stay open until you answer them. When the target you chose is reached by statements you have confirmed, the proposal is marked completed.

## At a glance

**Audience**

Anyone who has been told this is a note-taking tool and is wondering what it is actually for.

## Why it is this way

**Principle**

- EVERY OPTION SHOWS ITS WORKING. An option that cites three facts you recognise is one you can act on. An option that cites a fact you had forgotten is the useful kind of surprise. An option that cites nothing does not appear, because a proposal with no grounding is a guess with better formatting.
- IT CANNOT USE A FACT YOU HAVE NOT ACCEPTED. That is the entire difference between this and asking a chatbot the same question. A chatbot answers from everything it has ever read plus whatever you pasted; a reckoning answers from your confirmed graph and nothing else, which is why it can tell you it does not have enough to go on instead of producing a confident answer built on nothing.

**Constraint**

- IT IS ONLY AS GOOD AS WHAT YOU PUT IN, and the product does not pretend otherwise. A reckoning over a graph of nine facts will say very little of value. This is the honest cost of the approach: the review step that makes the answer trustworthy is also the step that makes it slow to get started, and no amount of automation removes it without also removing the reason to believe the result.
- IT PROPOSES. YOU DECIDE. A reckoning does not act, schedule, send, or change anything — it hands you options and the reasoning behind them. Nothing in this product does something on your behalf because it concluded that it should.

**Honest Note**

WHAT IS NOT BUILT OR NOT PROVEN YET. The card with the Accept button appears only in the session that generated the proposal; after a reload, use Show on the canvas, where the same buttons appear beside the option. If no generation tool is connected, accepting fails and nothing changes. Rejecting is final (a rejected option cannot be brought back), and rejecting every option does not mark the proposal failed. A job that fails later does not reopen anything. Answering a question about missing support takes plain text, not an upload. Accepting was checked only against a synthetic stand-in for the generation tool, never a real graphics-card job. Canvas selection takes whole things (everything that touches what you selected), not individual statements; the reckoning itself is not yet drawn or filtered specially on the canvas; and voice mode does not use the list.

## What we found

**Example**

SITUATION: our staging deploys have failed twice this month, both times after a dependency bump, and the person who set the pipeline up has left. TARGET: stop the failures without rebuilding the pipeline. What comes back is a handful of options assembled from what your graph already holds about those deploys — and each one names the facts it used, so you can see whether it read the situation correctly before you act on it.
